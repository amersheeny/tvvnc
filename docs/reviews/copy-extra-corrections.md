STRING: runButtonBody
KEYS: runButtonBody
ENGLISH: "This command may stop playback or change TV settings. Check the command name before sending."
VERDICT: APPROVE
REASONING: My earlier approval rested on the first sentence attributing the caution to the television, and the corrected fact — that the Sony remote-controller information returns only names and codes, with the danger judgement made locally by matching names such as reset, demo and forced — makes that attribution a false statement about where the warning came from, so the revision is required and this version is the right shape for it. What replaces the attribution is better than a hedge: the first sentence states the concrete consequence class without claiming any knowledge the app does not have, since "may" carries the uncertainty honestly, and the second sentence hands the reader the only evidence that actually exists, which is the command's own name displayed in the dialog. That second sentence is quietly doing the work the old attribution used to do, because it tells the reader what the judgement is based on without a clumsy clause explaining the app's matching rules, and it hands the decision to the person who knows their own television. I considered inserting the basis explicitly, along the lines of "this command's name suggests it may…", and rejected it: it repeats the word "name" three times inside a two-line modal body, and it makes the app's guesswork the subject of the sentence when the consequence is what the reader needs first. The noun "command" also matches the dialog title and the displayed value, so the whole dialog speaks about one object with one word.
RISKS: "May" must be rendered as genuine possibility, not as permission — the false-friend risk with modal verbs meaning "is allowed to" is live in several languages and would turn a warning into a grant. "Playback" needs the locale's television and streaming term rather than a generic word for reproduction. "Check the command name" means read and consider the name shown in this dialog, not verify it against an external list, and translators working from the string alone could easily produce the second sense.

STRING: secretsSaved
KEYS: secretsSaved
ENGLISH: "Credentials are stored securely on this phone and used only to connect to your TV."
VERDICT: REWORD
REWRITE: "Credentials are encrypted on this phone and used only to connect to this TV."
REASONING: My earlier approval of "Saved credentials stay on this phone" is void on the facts, not on anyone's preference: the pre-shared key and the session cookies are transmitted to the television, so a claim that the credentials stay on the phone was false, and the revision correctly abandons it. The proposal's replacement clause, "used only to connect to your TV", is the right solution and I am keeping it verbatim, including the verb "used" rather than "sent" — that choice is load-bearing, because the pre-shared key and cookies really are transmitted while the screen-sharing password is never sent at all but is proved through a challenge-and-response exchange, and "used" is the one verb that is true of both without describing either wrongly. Two things still need fixing. First, "stored securely" is a hollow intensifier of the kind every application prints and no reader can evaluate, whereas "encrypted" is the specific, already-established fact from the brief's own constraint list and is exactly the word this reader — a technically comfortable adult — can weigh. Second, "your TV" reintroduces the only second-person possessive in the whole string set, which I removed from the registration instruction for the same reason: this app is explicitly multi-television, the credential fields sit inside a single television's settings, and "this TV" names the one they are configuring while "your TV" names none of them in particular.
RISKS: "Encrypted" must use the locale's standard security term and must not drift into "encoded" or "compressed", which are different claims and would be inaccurate. "Used only to connect" carries the exclusivity that reassures the reader, so the restrictive "only" must attach to the purpose, not to the verb — a rendering meaning "merely used" instead of "used for nothing else" loses the entire point. "This TV" must be the demonstrative for the television currently being configured in the app, matching the registration instruction so the two strings address the reader the same way.

STRING: notConnected
KEYS: notConnected
ENGLISH: "Not connected to this TV. Choose Connect to send controls."
VERDICT: APPROVE
REASONING: My earlier rewrite pointed the reader at Reconnect, and the correction shows that was a factual error with real consequences — Reconnect in the preview area restores only the screen-sharing session, so a reader following my instruction after an explicit disconnect would have tapped a control that does not restore the console and would have concluded the app was broken. This version names the actual menu action, Connect, which is also the exact opposite of the Disconnect they used to get here, so the message and the menu form a matched pair the reader can navigate without hunting. The order is right as well: the state comes first, which explains the silence that prompted them to read the toast in the first place, and the instruction follows as a remedy rather than as an unexplained command. The verb "Choose" is consistent with the shortcut builder's "Choose an action" and avoids asserting a particular gesture for a menu item. I considered opening with "Disconnected from this TV", which would echo the action they took even more directly, and rejected it because "Not connected" stays true if this toast is ever shown in a state that was not reached through a deliberate disconnect, and robustness is worth more here than the extra echo.
RISKS: "Connect" is a user-interface label and must be translated identically to the menu item itself, so the two strings should be supplied to the translator together and marked as a matched pair; if they diverge, the message points at a control that does not exist under that name, which is the precise failure this revision fixes. Keep the capital on Connect, or use whatever convention the locale uses to mark a quoted control name, so the reader sees it as something to find rather than as an ordinary verb. "Send controls" means send control commands to the television, not dispatch physical handsets.

STRING: standbyIntent
KEYS: standbyIntent
ENGLISH: "After you turn the TV off here, use Turn on to resume the connection."
VERDICT: REWORD
REWRITE: "The app will not reconnect until you turn the TV on here, even if it is turned on another way."
REASONING: The proposal is accurate and names the right control, but it leaves out the single most surprising consequence of the behaviour the correction establishes — that the pause survives someone waking the panel with the television's own remote — and that is exactly the moment the reader will be confused: the picture is on, the app is idle, and nothing in the message they saw earlier prepared them for that combination. Stating it inside the same sentence costs six words and pre-empts the support question entirely. The second change is a phrasing one with a precedent already in this app: "use Turn on to resume the connection" stacks a verb phrase behind the verb "use", which reads as a stutter, while the already-approved power message says "Turn the TV on before sending other controls", so the construction "turn the TV on here" is both smoother and consistent with copy the reader has already met on the same screen, and it still contains the control's label words in order. I dropped the opening "After you turn the TV off here" because it restates the action the reader has just taken at the moment this line appears, and I named only the power control rather than also naming Connect, because Connect on a television that is switched off does not help — the reader needs one instruction, and it needs to be the one that works.
RISKS: "Here" means in this app as opposed to with the television's own remote, and that contrast is the whole sentence; a translation that drops it or renders it as a physical location destroys the meaning. "Turned on another way" must stay general enough to cover the television's own remote, its power button and a voice assistant, and must not be narrowed to a single device. Keep the concessive "even if" as a genuine concession — rendering it as a plain conditional would turn a warning into a condition the reader might think they can satisfy. This wording assumes the line is shown at or just after the moment the reader turns the television off; if it is placed somewhere the power-off is not obvious context, the opening clause the proposal used will need to be restored and the string re-reviewed.

RISKS: This must be translated identically to the direct-touch dialog title, the dialog body and the settings checkbox, so all four strings should travel to the translator as one linked set; if the diagnostics row drifts to a synonym, the reader cannot connect the failing feature to the row that reports on it. "Direct" here contrasts with the indirect, trackpad-style pointing of the Touchpad, and should not be rendered as "immediate" or "straightforward".

STRING: advertised
KEYS: advertised
ENGLISH: "Available to try — not tested yet"
VERDICT: APPROVE
REASONING: My earlier rewrite, "Reported by the TV", is wrong on the corrected facts and this version is right: the state can arise from a network hardware address the user typed in themselves for wake-on-network, from an affordance the screen-sharing protocol offers, or from flags negotiated during a session, and in the first of those cases the television announced nothing at all — so attributing the claim to the set would have been false on exactly the row where a reader is most likely to be debugging. "Available to try" is accurate across all three sources because it describes the app's readiness to attempt the thing rather than the provenance of the belief, and it satisfies the constraint that this state must never suggest a pending setup action, since it points forward at an attempt rather than back at a missing step. The two halves divide the work cleanly: the first tells the reader they can go ahead, the second preserves the distinction from the proven state, which is the whole reason this value exists. Scanning behaviour is good too, because the three states now begin with three visibly different words, and "Available to try" sits naturally beside the screen's own Test connections action, so the reader can see the row and the button that resolves it as two halves of one move.
RISKS: The em dash joining an availability clause to a status clause is not idiomatic everywhere and translators should be free to substitute a comma, a colon, a parenthetical or a line break. "Available to try" must not become "available on a trial basis", which in several languages is the more common reading of a literal translation and would imply a time-limited offer. "Not tested yet" means not yet exercised by this app, not failed and not unsupported, and the "yet" must survive — dropping it turns a neutral, actionable state into a verdict.
