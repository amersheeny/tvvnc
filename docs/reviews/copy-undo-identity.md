REASONING: The structural decision in this draft is right and is kept: putting the verb first, then the identifier, means the reader learns what happened before they have to process a name that could be anything, and the curly quotation marks correctly fence user-written text off from app-written text so that a shortcut called "Run" or "Delete" cannot be misread as part of the sentence. Placing the full stop outside the closing quotation mark is also deliberately correct and is preserved, because a full stop inside would look like part of the name the user chose. The defect is a missing noun, and the two supplied captures demonstrate it between them. On the Shortcuts screen the notification sits beneath a page titled Shortcuts, directly under a card reading "Local test shortcut" with "Steps: 1" beneath it, and in that setting the draft is perfectly clear. The second capture shows the same notification overlaying the Keyboard screen, covering the masked text field, and on that screen the word "shortcut" appears nowhere at all — not in the app bar, not in the connection row, not on any control. A reader who has navigated away and sees "Deleted “Netflix evening”." has no way to tell what kind of thing was deleted, and this app makes that question live rather than academic, because it deliberately uses different verbs for different objects: televisions are forgotten, shortcuts are deleted, text is cleared, favourites are removed. Since the second line of this notification names a television, a reader could reasonably assemble "Deleted “Netflix evening” … Parents TV" into the belief that something has happened to the saved television. Adding one word removes that reading entirely and costs eleven characters. The word "shortcut" rather than any synonym is required, since it is the noun on the page title, on the Add shortcut button, on the shortcut name field, and in the existing notification visible in both captures. The redundancy this introduces on the Shortcuts screen itself was weighed and accepted: a single repeated noun in the one place where context is already strong is a much smaller cost than an unidentifiable object in the place where context is gone, and the notification's whole purpose is to survive the move between those two screens.

Reading off the default-size capture, the notification's text region holds roughly 48 characters before it meets the Undo action; the rewritten line with the example name in the capture, "Deleted shortcut “Local test shortcut”.", is 39 characters and fits with little to spare. For screen-reader users the quotation marks are generally not announced, so the line is heard as "Deleted shortcut Local test shortcut" — which is exactly why the class noun in front of the name matters, since without it the announcement is a bare name with no category. Looking ahead to any language other than English, the placeholder holds a proper name that may be in a different script from the surrounding sentence, so it will need bidirectional isolation to keep the quotation marks and the full stop on the correct side of the name in a right-to-left layout.

STRING: shortcutDeletionContext
KEYS: shortcutDeletionContext
ENGLISH: "Deletion {number} · {tv}"
VERDICT: APPROVE
REASONING: This line has one job that nothing else in the notification can do — letting the reader tell two deletions apart when the names are identical — and it does that job with two independent handles rather than one. The television name answers the question a reader asks after navigating away, which the second capture shows is a real situation rather than a hypothetical, since the notification is rendered there on top of an unrelated screen. The number answers the question the television name cannot, which is which of two same-named shortcuts on the same television this particular Undo will restore. The choice of separator is well judged and is already established in the product: the connection row in both captures reads "VNC · Connected", so the middle dot is the app's own way of joining two identifying facts. It is also more truthful than any preposition available. "Deletion 2 on Parents TV" would say the deletion happened on the television, which is false — it happened in the phone app — and "from Parents TV" would imply the shortcut was removed from the television itself rather than from the app's saved list. A separator asserts no relationship at all and simply offers two labels for the same item, which is precisely the claim that can be supported. "Deletion" is the right noun because it is the noun form of the app's own Delete control, the trash-can icon visible on the shortcut card in the first capture, so the word is derived from the product rather than invented beside it. The numbering pattern reads as an identifier by the same familiar analogy as Step 2 or Page 2, and the brief's own worry that a reader might take it as a count of shortcuts deleted is answered by the line above it, which names exactly one shortcut in the singular — the two lines interlock, and the rewrite approved above strengthens that protection by making the singular object explicit. One further property makes the identifier trustworthy: because the numbering restarts only when the app restarts, and restarting also clears every pending notification, two visible notifications can never carry the same number. Whether to number deletions at all was settled by the interface specialist and is explicitly outside this review; what is inside it is whether the words naming that number can be understood, and they can.
RISKS: Number handling and length. A screen reader will announce the numeral as a cardinal, so this line is heard as "Deletion two Parents TV", with the middle dot silent and no grammatical join between the two facts — parseable, and consistent with how the existing "VNC · Connected" row already behaves, but it depends on the line above having established that exactly one shortcut is in question. Length is the sharper risk: measured from the large-text capture, roughly 23 characters fit on a line, and "Deletion 2 · Parents TV" is 23 characters exactly, so any television name longer than the example will wrap at that text size, on a notification that is already showing two text lines and two action affordances over the top of another screen. The television name is user-written and unbounded, which makes wrapping likely rather than possible. If the numbering ever reaches three digits in a long session the line grows by a further two characters. For a future right-to-left layout, a line that mixes a Latin-script numeral, a middle dot and a proper name needs bidirectional isolation around both placeholders, or the number and the name can visually swap order and the identifier stops identifying.

STRING: dismiss
KEYS: dismiss
ENGLISH: "Dismiss"
VERDICT: APPROVE
REASONING: This is the correct word and, importantly, it is correct largely because of what it is not. The button sits directly beside Undo, and the single most damaging thing this label could do is read as a second way to reverse the deletion or as a way to confirm it. "Cancel" would have been actively dangerous here, since in a notification about something that has just been deleted a reader would reasonably take "Cancel" to mean cancel the deletion, which is Undo's job and not this control's. "Close" would describe the icon accurately but collides with the app's other closing actions, such as leaving full screen or hiding the screen, and it says nothing about the thing being closed. "Dismiss" is the conventional word for making a transient message go away without acting on its contents, it carries no suggestion of restoring anything, and it will already be familiar to the reader from notifications elsewhere on their phone. It also stands alone correctly for a screen-reader user, who hears "Dismiss, button" with no surrounding context needed, which matters because the captures show this notification appearing on screens that have nothing to do with shortcuts. The label does not explain that dismissing reveals the next queued deletion, and it should not: that behaviour explains itself the moment it happens, and spending words on it would lengthen a line that the large-text capture shows has almost no room left. The one thing this label cannot do is tell the reader that dismissing forfeits the chance to undo, but no single-word action label could, and the presence of Undo immediately beside it makes the alternative visible at the moment of choice.
RISKS: None in the wording itself. One layout consequence is worth recording, since it follows from adding this control rather than from the word chosen: in the default-size capture the notification's text runs to roughly 48 characters before reaching Undo, and this close-icon button will take a further slice of that width, tightening both lines above. In the large-text capture, where only about 23 characters fit per line and the notification already covers the user's text entry field, the combination of two wrapped text lines, Undo and a Dismiss icon will make this bar noticeably tall. There is no number, no gender and no directional term in this string, so it carries no right-to-left or pluralization exposure of its own.
